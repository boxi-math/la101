Linear Programming and DualityNo lecture of its own: this topic belongs to the optimisation course OPT101

A linear program optimises a linear function over a region cut out by linear inequalities. LA101 meets it twice: maximum flows in a network (elective E3) and optimal mixed strategies in a zero-sum game (E3, part 2) are both linear programs, and max-flow min-cut and the minimax theorem are both instances of linear programming duality.

A linear program in two variables

A workshop makes tables (\(x\)) and chairs (\(y\)) and earns 3 per table and 5 per chair. It has 4 hours of cutting and 6 hours of finishing; a table takes 1 hour of each, a chair 1 hour of cutting and 3 of finishing:

$$\text{maximise } 3x + 5y \quad\text{subject to}\quad x + y \le 4,\quad x + 3y \le 6,\quad x \ge 0,\ y \ge 0 .$$

The allowed region is a polygon with corners \((0, 0)\), \((4, 0)\), \((3, 1)\) and \((0, 2)\). A linear objective is largest at a corner: the values are 0, 12, 14 and 10, so the best plan is \((3, 1)\) with profit 14. Finding a corner means solving the square linear system of the constraints that are tight there, here \(x + y = 4\), \(x + 3y = 6\): elimination, Lecture 2.

The dual program

Put a price \(u\) on an hour of cutting and \(v\) on an hour of finishing. If the hours a table uses are worth at least 3 and the hours a chair uses at least 5, the hours themselves are worth at least as much as any plan earns:

$$\text{minimise } 4u + 6v \quad\text{subject to}\quad u + v \ge 3,\quad u + 3v \ge 5,\quad u \ge 0,\ v \ge 0 .$$

Any plan earns at most any such pricing (weak duality), so every feasible pricing is a certificate. Both dual constraints are tight at \(u = 2\), \(v = 1\), and \(4 \cdot 2 + 6 \cdot 1 = 14\): the dual minimum equals the primal maximum (strong duality). The prices say what one more hour of cutting (2) or of finishing (1) is worth. The dual uses the transposed matrix, \(A^\top\), the same transpose that links the column space and the row space in Lecture 12.
